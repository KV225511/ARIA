\begin{abstract}
Adaptive mock interviews require decisions about which evidence to collect as well as how to interpret an answer. We present ARIA, an architecture that connects calibrated skill beliefs to an offline Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) policy through a 33-dimensional state and eight interview actions. The evaluation separates historical component baselines, a locked synthetic belief test, and direct development comparisons against behavior cloning, discrete Conservative Q-Learning, a Decision-Transformer-inspired model, and four heuristic policies. On 91 locked episodes, the v7 belief configuration improves accuracy from 0.7912 to 0.9121 and macro-F1 from 0.8010 to 0.9168, although expected calibration error increases slightly. The policy comparison uses 45 matched episodes in each of four constructed score conditions. Base accuracy reaches 1.0000 for seven policies and cannot rank them. With overlapping evidence, IQL achieves accuracy 0.4667, compared with 0.5333 for behavior cloning and 0.5778 for uncertainty greedy. IQL nevertheless has the largest mean designed return and information gain among the learned policies across all conditions and covers all 17 skills. These findings show a coverage and reward advantage within the specified simulator, rather than uniformly better classification. The study documents the comparator adaptations, distinguishes training data from generated rollout evidence, and identifies incomplete live integration and absent human validation as limits on end-to-end claims.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} adaptive interviewing; skill beliefs; offline reinforcement learning; implicit Q-learning; synthetic evaluation; baseline comparison.

\section{Introduction}
A practice interview is a sequence of decisions about what evidence to collect. An interviewer may stay with a topic when an answer is incomplete, revisit a prerequisite when a response exposes a gap, or move on when the available evidence is sufficient. These choices influence both the final assessment and the time required to obtain it. A system that generates plausible questions therefore addresses only part of the problem: it also needs a way to represent uncertainty and decide which question serves the interview next.

ARIA, an Autonomous Reinforcement-Based Interview Agent, treats these decisions as a sequential control problem. The architecture connects role and skill context to a probabilistic belief model and an offline policy. A fixed state representation exposes the current evidence, coverage, and recent interaction history, while a validity mask restricts the actions available at each turn. The intended application is mock-interview practice. The competence labels used in the experiments are synthetic categories rather than independently validated measures of professional ability.

Existing research offers several relevant but distinct precedents. HireNet predicts recruiter judgments from recorded interviews \cite{hirenet}. SimInterview describes personalized interview practice using language models and retrieval \cite{siminterview}, while InterPilot supports human interviewers during an interview \cite{interpilot}. ARIA examines a narrower question: how an explicit belief state and an offline action policy behave when they share a controlled assessment interface. Personalization or multimodal input, by itself, is not claimed as a new contribution.

The present evaluation combines three sources of evidence. Archived component experiments compare response-processing methods on public benchmark tasks. A locked synthetic evaluation compares two belief configurations. A subsequent development experiment trains policy comparators on ARIA's stored transitions and evaluates them in matched synthetic rollouts. Keeping these sources distinct matters because their labels, sampling units, and outcomes differ. A component classifier can improve without improving interview decisions, and a policy can obtain a higher designed reward without classifying candidates more accurately.

We address three research questions. RQ1 asks what the recorded component baselines establish about the value of the available response signals. RQ2 asks whether the frozen v7 belief configuration improves synthetic classification over v6 on the locked split. RQ3 asks how the existing IQL policy compares with learned and heuristic alternatives under the same development rollout interface, including conditions with ambiguous or weak evidence.

The contribution is a documented belief-to-action architecture and a layered empirical analysis of that architecture. The direct comparisons add behavioral evidence beyond a single accuracy score, including information gain, coverage, and interview length. They reveal a trade-off between IQL's coverage-oriented behavior and the classification performance of simpler policies. The study also identifies the limits of a score-based simulator and of compact adaptations of published algorithms. These limits define the scope of the findings rather than being deferred to a claim of general superiority.

\section{Related work and state-of-the-art analysis}
\subsection{Interview assessment and interactive support}
HireNet models the structure of question--answer sequences using hierarchical attention and predicts recruiter-provided hirability judgments \cite{hirenet}. This makes it relevant to the representation of interview evidence, but its prediction target differs from the choice of an interview action. A direct comparison would require compatible labels and inputs, not merely an accuracy column beside ARIA's results.

Recent systems broaden the interaction setting. SimInterview uses language models and retrieval to organize personalized practice around applicant and job information \cite{siminterview}. InterPilot instead supplies real-time assistance to a human interviewer, including questions and evidence-oriented support \cite{interpilot}. These examples place ARIA within a field that already includes adaptive practice and interviewer assistance. They are systems-level references, not models trained in the experiments reported here.

MAG-BERT-ARL combines multimodal language representations with adversarial reweighting to study fairness in automated video interview assessment \cite{mag}. Its emphasis on underrepresented groups highlights a separate evaluation requirement. Neither multimodal inputs nor a confidence score demonstrate that a system treats candidate groups comparably. ARIA's synthetic policy experiments do not contain the population evidence needed to answer that question.

\subsection{Offline policies and calibration}
Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) learns values from logged state--action pairs and extracts a policy by advantage-weighted fitting \cite{iql}. Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) adds a penalty to reduce optimistic action-value estimates in offline learning \cite{cql}. Decision Transformer treats control as return-conditioned sequence prediction \cite{dt}. These are established algorithmic precedents with different training objectives. Their original benchmark results cannot be transferred to an interview simulator.

We therefore distinguish algorithm provenance from implementation fidelity. The experiments use the existing ARIA IQL checkpoint, a behavior-cloning baseline, a compact discrete CQL implementation, and a return-conditioned causal Transformer inspired by Decision Transformer. The last comparator combines several embeddings at each time step and differs from the published tokenization. None of the newly trained comparators is an imported, author-supplied interview system. Their results assess these local implementations under ARIA's interface.

Calibration is also distinct from discrimination. A classifier may become more accurate while the probabilities it reports become less well aligned with observed frequencies. Guo et al. study this distinction for modern neural networks \cite{calibration}. Accordingly, the locked evaluation reports both classification and probability-quality measures. For offline policy evaluation, Thomas et al. formulate confidence bounds on expected policy return \cite{ope}; ARIA's transition-level weighting diagnostic does not implement that guarantee.

\section{ARIA architecture and learning formulation}
\subsection{Context, evidence, and skill beliefs}
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} presents the architecture and its implementation boundary. Resume and job-description context identify relevant skills and guide question construction. Candidate responses can be represented through semantic and optional acoustic or visual processing. The belief module receives an evidence score together with confidence and availability information. Keeping this interface explicit allows the policy to operate on compact state features without consuming raw recordings.

The current competency likelihood depends on semantic evidence. A behavioral score remains in the interface for compatibility but is not included in that likelihood. Emotion recognition and the historical deception task are therefore component investigations, not validated proxies for competence in the policy study. Missing or weak evidence is represented through reliability factors rather than silently converted into a confident skill judgment.

For each skill k, the model maintains a probability distribution over beginner, intermediate, and expert categories. Given semantic score x at a turn, the class-specific Gaussian log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
\ell_c(x_t)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x_t-\mu_c}{\sigma_c}\right)^2-\log\sigma_c.
\end{equation}
The omitted Gaussian constant is shared across classes. The update can be written in normalized form as
\begin{equation}
b_{k,t+1}(c)=\frac{b_{k,t}(c)\exp\{w_t\ell_c(x_t)\}}{\sum_j b_{k,t}(j)\exp\{w_t\ell_j(x_t)\}}.
\end{equation}
The weight w represents the evidence-strength adjustment used by the implementation. Lower confidence reduces the influence of an observation. Coverage and reporting checks determine whether accumulated evidence is sufficient to emit a label. This distinction between holding a posterior and having enough evidence to report it is part of the assessment interface.

\subsection{Policy state and action constraints}
The policy observes 33 features: five global belief summaries, three progress features, five features for the focused skill, eight summaries of recent evidence, eight indicators of the previous action, and four channel availability features. The representation has a fixed size even when the underlying skill context changes. It summarizes the interview rather than preserving the full response history, which makes learning tractable but can discard distinctions between histories with similar summaries.

The action set contains eight choices: increase difficulty, decrease difficulty, ask a follow-up on the same topic, switch topic, probe a foundation, ask a behavioral question, ask a situational question, and conclude the interview. An external validity mask identifies available actions before selection. The mask is not counted among the 33 state features. All policies in the direct comparison use this action interface and the same final belief aggregation, so classification differences arise through the evidence collected rather than through separate downstream classifiers.

The offline environment implements the belief update, masking, and action loop. The live application is less complete: its current response path supplies fixed evidence scores and cycles through three actions. The offline results therefore evaluate the environment and frozen checkpoints. They do not demonstrate that the deployed interaction already selects questions through the learned policy or that raw audio and video are calibrated into the tested scores.

\subsection{Reward and IQL objective}
The policy objective is discounted return,
\begin{equation}
J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right],\qquad\gamma=0.99.
\end{equation}
The implemented step reward gives 1.5 times nonnegative information gain, a 0.5 bonus for the first visit to a skill, and a 0.1 time cost. Repeated visits incur an additional penalty that increases by 0.05 per previous visit up to 0.15. Anxiety and invalid-action penalties are 1.0 and 0.5, respectively. A valid stop has zero immediate step reward. Other coefficients retained in the configuration are not treated as active terms unless the reward function uses them.

The replay construction can additionally apply a terminal outcome adjustment once: +5 for the correct class, -2.5 for an adjacent class, -5 for a two-level error, and -1.25 for no prediction. This uses the known synthetic label during data construction. The direct rollout harness does not add this ground-truth terminal adjustment to the summed environment rewards. Its reported designed return therefore reflects the runtime evidence, coverage, and cost terms, not a separately awarded correctness bonus. Logged training return and rollout return must be interpreted with this difference in mind.

ARIA uses twin action-value networks, a state-value network, and a policy network. The value loss weights positive and negative residuals asymmetrically:
\begin{equation}
L_V=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim D}[|\tau-\mathbf{1}(u<0)|u^2],\qquad u=\min_j\bar Q_j(s,a)-V(s).
\end{equation}
The frozen configuration uses expectile 0.8. Policy extraction minimizes
\begin{equation}
L_\pi=-\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim D}[\min\{\exp(\beta A(s,a)),100\}\log\pi(a\mid s)],\qquad\beta=3.
\end{equation}
These objectives follow the IQL learning pattern \cite{iql}; the architecture and hyperparameters described here are the repository implementation. The existing checkpoint is held fixed throughout this revision and the direct comparisons.

\subsection{Learned comparators and training protocol}
Behavior cloning predicts the logged action using cross-entropy. Its network maps the 33-dimensional state through two hidden layers of width 128 to eight outputs, with normalization and rectified-linear activations. Training uses the recorded actions; validation and rollout selection apply the legal-action mask. This provides a useful imitation baseline without a learned return objective.

The discrete CQL comparator uses a network of the same width. It combines a temporal-difference loss with a conservative penalty formed from the log-sum-exp of legal action values minus the value of the logged action. The penalty coefficient is 1.0 and the discount is 0.99. A target network is synchronized once per epoch. This is a compact discrete adaptation of the CQL principle \cite{cql}, not a reproduction of every training choice in the original study.

The DT-inspired comparator uses a causal Transformer with two layers, four attention heads, width 128, and context length 12. State, previous-action, return-to-go, and position embeddings are summed into one token at each time step. Training uses undiscounted remaining logged rewards, scaled by the maximum absolute training-episode return, and an action loss at the last valid position of each context. The rollout target starts from the 90th percentile of training-episode returns. These choices produce a return-conditioned sequence baseline, but the combined token differs from the separate return, state, and action tokens of Decision Transformer \cite{dt}.

There is also a consequential implementation limitation: training contexts contain a separate remaining return for each historical time step, whereas inference repeats the current remaining target across the context. Together with the difference between logged terminal shaping and rollout rewards, this complicates the interpretation of the Transformer's low return. The present paper reports the observed implementation faithfully; it does not infer that the published algorithm is intrinsically unsuitable for interviewing.

All three new comparators use seed 42, 20 epochs, batches of 256, and a learning rate of 0.0003. The selected checkpoint maximizes validation logged-action accuracy across 1,405 transitions. The frozen IQL network instead has hidden width 256 and its own earlier training and selection procedure. Thus, the comparison matches data access and rollout conditions, but not parameter count, tuning effort, or selection objective. In particular, imitation accuracy is an imperfect selection criterion for reward-oriented methods.

Four non-learned policies complete the comparison: random valid actions, a fixed script, a rule-based adaptive policy, and an uncertainty-greedy policy. They expose whether a learned policy improves over simple behavior under the same evidence and stopping constraints. They receive the same shared belief machinery as the learned policies. Their inclusion is especially useful when class separation makes almost any sequence of valid questions sufficient for correct classification.

\subsection{Matched development rollouts}
Each condition contains 45 episodes, balanced across the three synthetic classes, and each of the eight policies is evaluated on those scenarios. There are 1,440 policy--scenario runs across four conditions. The scenario identities recur across conditions; these are not 1,440 independent candidates. Each rollout uses the default 17-skill ontology and a maximum of 30 turns.

The score provider begins with the center assigned to the known synthetic class. It adds an episode-level offset, a turn-level perturbation, any global shift, and a 0.025 bonus for a follow-up or foundation probe, then clips the score to the unit interval. Perturbations are deterministic functions of the scenario and action context. The true class is used by the generator and evaluator, not passed directly as an input to the policy. Behavioral score is fixed at 0.5; the provider occasionally assigns the anxiety flag used by the reward.

Matching therefore means that every policy faces the same scenario specification and deterministic response rule. Different actions can produce different scores because the rule includes the action, turn, and targeted skill. Policies do not necessarily observe identical realized evidence. This is a controlled intervention within a simulator, not a replay of the same human answers to alternative questions.

\subsection{Outcomes, uncertainty, and provenance}
Classification accuracy compares the final aggregate belief label with the synthetic class. Macro-F1 gives each class equal weight. Mean designed return is the sum of rollout rewards. Cumulative information gain sums nonnegative decreases in target-skill entropy, each weighted by the increase in effective evidence count. Turn count and distinct skills visited describe the cost and breadth of evidence collection. Because information gain and coverage appear directly in the reward, agreement among these metrics is partly built into the objective and is not independent validation.

The canonical rollout reports use 2,000 episode-bootstrap resamples for their intervals. We retain the archived reward intervals in Appendix A. They describe scenario variation conditional on one fitted checkpoint; they do not quantify training-seed variability. A bootstrap accuracy interval can collapse to [1,1] when every sampled episode is correct. Such a result reflects the finite, saturated sample and is not proof of perfect population performance.

The locked belief report includes a paired cluster analysis over only six linked-identity components. The report marks that analysis as diagnostic. We preserve this qualification rather than turning its intervals into a broad confirmatory claim. Likewise, no significance ranking is inferred from overlapping or non-overlapping marginal rollout intervals. Canonical per-episode traces are not included in the compact summary used for this redraft, so no new paired tests are calculated from locally available traces belonging to a different run.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Historical component baselines}
Table~\ref{tab:components} reports the recoverable component results. On Mohler short-answer grading, the hybrid method has the highest listed accuracy, 0.7516, but the linear SVM has the highest macro-F1, 0.6030. The ordering changes with the metric. This favors reporting both measures rather than describing a single model as uniformly best.

Audio and visual results are lower when used alone. This observation concerns those implementations and the recorded split; it is not evidence that audio or video can never help an interview system. It does show why additional modalities need an explicit contribution analysis before being treated as useful evidence.

\subsection{Offline weighting diagnostic}
The release also records a validation-only transition weighting estimate. With clipped importance ratios, the implemented statistic is
\begin{equation}
\rho_i=\min\left\{\frac{\pi(a_i\mid s_i)}{\mu(a_i\mid s_i)},20\right\},\qquad
\widehat R=\frac{\sum_i\rho_i r_i}{\sum_i\rho_i},\qquad
\mathrm{ESS}=\frac{(\sum_i\rho_i)^2}{\sum_i\rho_i^2}.
\end{equation}
The reported value is 0.4618, with effective sample size approximately 1,003.5 and maximum ratio approximately 5.464. This is a weighted one-step reward diagnostic over validation transitions. It does not estimate a complete interview return with a trajectory-level uncertainty guarantee, and its numerical scale should not be compared with the cumulative rollout returns above. The release's deployment gate remains false.

\section{Discussion}
The evaluation demonstrates why the unit of analysis must be explicit in an adaptive interview study. The locked belief experiment measures how a fixed evidence processor labels stored episodes. The direct policy experiment measures which evidence an action policy collects from a constructed response rule. The historical component tests measure still other tasks. Together they describe different parts of the architecture, but they do not add up automatically to an end-to-end human evaluation.

The central behavioral result is the separation between coverage-oriented return and classification. IQL reliably spreads its questions across the default skill set and obtains more information gain under the defined update. Under overlapping evidence, however, greater accumulated information is not enough to give it the most accurate final label. An entropy reduction can accompany a mistaken belief when the likelihood is misspecified. Rewarding uncertainty reduction therefore requires separate checks of correctness and calibration.

The base accuracy ceiling is also instructive. It is not, on its own, proof of leakage or a coding failure. The score generator assigns widely separated centers to the known classes, and the common belief updater is designed to recover those distinctions. At the same time, perfect performance in this environment is a weak test of adaptive interviewing. The result identifies a property of the generator--updater combination, not evidence that arbitrary candidate responses can be assessed perfectly.

The direct comparators improve the study by placing several learning approaches behind the same interface. They do not establish that IQL is superior to the full published CQL or Decision Transformer methods. Capacity and checkpoint selection differ, only one training seed is available, and the Transformer has documented representation and inference limitations. The most defensible statement is that the frozen IQL checkpoint obtains higher mean simulator return than the particular comparators trained here.

Finally, the component results favor restraint in the architecture's multimodal claims. The strongest recorded MOSEI accuracy comes from text alone, and the competency likelihood in the current offline evaluation is semantic. Audio and video may still support feedback or interaction, but their contribution to accurate skill assessment remains unmeasured in this loop. This observation connects the baseline analysis to a concrete design question: which signal changes an assessment decision in a way that is useful and justified?

\section{Limitations and research implications}
The first limitation is construct validity. The synthetic classes and class-conditioned score generator simplify the relationship between answers and competence. Skills within an episode share a latent class, and fixed action bonuses encode assumptions about the value of particular questions. The evaluator does not measure relevance, factual correctness, conversational coherence, learning benefit, or candidate experience from generated dialogue. Those properties require evidence beyond scalar scores.

The second limitation is experimental breadth. The new policy results use one training seed and 45 episodes per condition, with stress conditions chosen after observing a ceiling. Matching reduces differences in scenario assignment but does not account for uncertainty in the response model itself. The locked belief test contains only six identity components, which limits the stability of cluster-based inference. No broad population or confirmatory significance claim follows from these samples.

The third limitation concerns comparison fidelity. The frozen IQL checkpoint and newly trained baselines do not share an identical tuning budget. Validation action accuracy may select a good imitator rather than the best return-maximizing policy. The DT-inspired implementation also differs materially from its published predecessor, and the replay reward includes terminal shaping absent from the reported online reward sum. These are reasons to qualify algorithm-level conclusions, not to suppress the measured results.

The fourth limitation is evidence completeness. Canonical compact summaries preserve result values and hashes, but the corresponding per-episode artifacts are needed for new paired analyses. Historical component metrics rely on summaries without recovered prediction files. The current live application does not execute the entire evaluated adaptive loop. Consequently, neither the component benchmarks nor the synthetic policy experiment establishes a deployable automated assessment system.

For future evaluation, the same frozen model could be tested across additional scenario seeds and externally specified score conditions without changing its training data. Stronger evidence would also separate heterogeneous skill profiles, question usefulness, calibration under missing modalities, and candidate-facing outcomes. Any study involving people would need an explicit protocol, consent and data-handling arrangements, and relevant approval where required. No such study or approval is claimed here. Fairness, accessibility, and adverse effects remain empirical questions, especially when behavioral signals are involved.

\section{Conclusion}
ARIA provides an explicit interface between skill beliefs and offline interview actions. The revised evaluation shows improved synthetic belief classification for v7, identifies useful historical component baselines, and compares the frozen IQL policy with learned and heuristic alternatives under matched development conditions. IQL obtains the largest mean designed return and information gain among the learned policies across all four conditions, while simpler alternatives classify better when evidence overlaps. The result is evidence of a simulator-specific coverage and reward advantage, with clear limits on claims about assessment quality. A complete live integration and externally grounded evaluation are needed before extending that conclusion to real interviews.
